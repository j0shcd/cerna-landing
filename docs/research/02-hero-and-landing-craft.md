## 1. Distilled principles

*Guidance distilled from practitioners; do/don’t examples below are illustrative.*

1. **Make the promise tangible.** Concrete work gives readers something to picture. **Do:** “Less time sorting transactions.” **Don’t:** “Financial clarity, reimagined.” — [Harry Dry](https://marketingexamples.com/landing-page/labels)

2. **Borrow the customer’s language.** Recognition starts with how people describe their problem. **Do:** investigate “keeping my spreadsheet up to date.” **Don’t:** manufacture anxiety about “financial chaos.” — [Joanna Wiebe](https://copyhackers.com/write-compelling-agitation-copy/)

3. **Match awareness.** Someone already tracking expenses needs a better way, not a lecture about tracking. **Do:** explain the replacement workflow. **Don’t:** explain why money matters. — [Schwartz’s publisher](https://breakthroughadvertisingbook.com/)

4. **Answer skepticism with mechanism.** Market sophistication concerns promises people have already encountered. **Do:** explain what gets automated. **Don’t:** intensify “effortless” into “revolutionary.” — [Schwartz’s publisher](https://breakthroughadvertisingbook.com/mastering-market-sophistication-the-key-to-winning-in-any-industry/)

5. **Find the persuasive product detail.** A distinguishing fact can carry the argument. **Do:** show duplicate detection working. **Don’t:** declare “unmatched precision.” — [Ogilvy’s campaign archive](https://www.ogilvy.com/ideas/ogilvy-75-75-years-iconic-campaigns)

6. **Give each line a different job.** Sugarman describes copy as a sequence that earns continued reading. **Do:** promise → explanation → evidence. **Don’t:** repeat the promise three times. — [Sugarman interview](https://josephbushnell.com/the-online-marketing-show/joe-sugarman/)

7. **Start with existing demand.** Halbert prioritises demonstrated appetite over rhetorical ingenuity. **Do:** address people maintaining expense spreadsheets. **Don’t:** target “everyone who wants financial freedom.” — [Gary Halbert](https://www.thegaryhalbertletter.com/newsletters/direct_marketing_to_a_starving_crowd.htm)

8. **Reframe the benefit.** Sutherland explores how changing interpretation changes perceived value. **Do:** sell relief from upkeep. **Don’t:** assume another dashboard is inherently desirable. — [Rory Sutherland](https://www.ted.com/talks/rory_sutherland_perspective_is_everything?view=transcript)

9. **Provide a reference point.** Visitors need to recognise what they have found. **Do:** name expense tracking somewhere in the hero. **Don’t:** leave them decoding “Your next chapter.” — [FletchPMM](https://www.fletchpmm.com/newsletter/how-to-write-an-h1)

10. **Keep the hero economical, not cryptic.** Shapiro recommends a descriptive headline and one or two supporting sentences. **Do:** explain the mechanism underneath. **Don’t:** force everything into five words. — [Julian Shapiro](https://www.julian.com/guide/startup/landing-pages)

11. **Connect benefits to capabilities.** Benefits alone can become interchangeable. **Do:** pair “less upkeep” with the operations removed. **Don’t:** stack “confidence, freedom, peace.” — [Laudi and Suellentrop](https://forgetthefunnel.com/resources/ep-18-features-vs-benefits)

## 3. Six less-obvious opportunities

*All are inference.*

- **Sell maintenance disappearing.** Show the recurring chore removed, rather than another destination demanding attention.
- **Respect existing competence.** “You already track your money” may create recognition without remedial budgeting language.
- **Demonstrate an exception.** One duplicate correctly caught could establish more credibility than a pristine dashboard.
- **Show the handoff.** Make clear where automation finishes and the owner decides.
- **Make privacy behavioural.** Explain where information goes and what the owner controls; avoid relying on a shield icon.
- **Let the page end.** A convincing demonstration and answered objections may remove the need for ten feature sections.

## 4. Failure modes and AI-sounding tells

*Editorial judgments—inference.*

Avoid interchangeable abstractions: “unlock financial freedom,” “seamless clarity,” “effortlessly empowered.” Avoid repetitive three-part slogans, forced “not X, but Y” constructions, fake urgency, and decorative statistics.

“Life is not meant to be lived in spreadsheets” recognises frustration, but takes a long route to the benefit. It can also sound dismissive of a tool this audience deliberately chose.

“Your finances on autopilot” risks implying autonomous investing, payments, or financial judgment. A waitlist label does not resolve that ambiguity.

## 5. Implications for Cerna

*Everything below is inference and proposed copy.*

**Recommended hero**

> **Stay on top of your money.  
> Spend less time tracking it.**
>
> Cerna handles the tedious parts of expense tracking—from importing statements to sorting transactions and converting currencies—so you can see where you stand and get on with life.
>
> **Join the waitlist**

**More distinctive alternative:** “Your money deserves attention. Your spreadsheet doesn’t need a second job.” Test comprehension carefully; the recommended version is clearer.

**Length:** start with roughly 10–14 headline words and 25–40 supporting words. These are drafting constraints, not scientifically established optima.

**Structure:** hero and waitlist → one real statement-to-overview demonstration → three chores removed → privacy and owner control → short founder explanation → practical FAQ → repeated waitlist.

**Pre-launch proof:** use a working capture, a traceable example, plainly stated supported imports, and an identifiable builder. These demonstrate capability and accountability; they cannot substitute for evidence of customer outcomes.

Explain statement importing early. Hold “autopilot” as the broader ambition until the page makes the remaining owner effort unmistakable.

**Next step:** test whether intended users can explain what Cerna does, what they still do, and what joining means. Research only; no files changed or commit created.
